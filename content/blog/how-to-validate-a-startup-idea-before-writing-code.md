Most solo founders don't fail because they build badly. They fail because they build something nobody was waiting for, and they find out after months of evenings and weekends.

The usual advice is "talk to customers." That's right, but it's incomplete. Talking without a clear hypothesis turns into collecting compliments. You need something that can actually be proven wrong.

This guide walks through the method I use: turn the idea into a **falsifiable hypothesis**, decide in advance what would kill it, then go find evidence.

## Why "validation" usually doesn't validate anything

Here's what weak validation looks like:

- You describe the idea to ten people and seven say "I'd use that."
- A landing page gets a few dozen signups from a post in a friendly community.
- A competitor exists, so "there's clearly a market."

None of these can fail. A polite "I'd use that" costs nothing to say. A signup is not a payment. A competitor proves somebody has the problem, not that they'd switch to you.

If no result could have changed your mind, you weren't validating. You were looking for permission.

## Step 1: Write the idea as a five-part hypothesis

Break your idea into five clauses. Each one is a separate bet, and each can be wrong on its own.

| Clause | Question it answers | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Segment** | Who exactly has this problem? | Freelance video editors working with agency clients |
| **Problem** | What hurts, in their words? | Client revision rounds eat several unpaid hours a week |
| **Alternative** | What do they do today instead? | Email threads, timestamped notes in Google Docs |
| **Reason** | Why is the alternative not good enough? | Feedback is scattered, so edits get missed and redone |
| **Willingness to pay** | What would they pay, and how? | $15–30/month, paid by the editor, not the client |

Write it as one sentence:

> Freelance video editors with agency clients lose hours each week to revision rounds, currently manage feedback in email and docs, find that edits get missed because feedback is scattered, and would pay $15–30/month for a tool that fixes it.

Notice how specific this is. "Creators who want better workflows" can't be tested. "Freelance video editors with agency clients" can — you know where to find them and what to ask.

## Step 2: Set kill conditions before you look at any evidence

This is the step almost everyone skips, and it's the one that matters most.

For each clause, write down the result that would make you drop or change it. Do it **before** you start interviews or research, while you're still neutral.

- **Segment:** I can't find 15 people matching this description within a week.
- **Problem:** Fewer than 4 of 10 interviewees mention revisions as a top-three frustration without being prompted.
- **Alternative:** Most people say their current setup "works fine."
- **Reason:** Nobody can describe a recent, specific time the current approach failed them.
- **Willingness to pay:** Nobody has paid for any tool to solve this, and nobody agrees to a paid pilot.

Pre-registering kill conditions protects you from your own optimism. After three hopeful conversations, it's very easy to reinterpret a weak signal as a strong one.

## Step 3: Test the riskiest clause first

You don't need to test all five clauses at once. Ask: *which clause, if wrong, kills the whole idea?*

For most technical founders, it's **Problem** or **Willingness to pay**. Builders tend to fall in love with a solution and assume the pain is obvious.

Start there and spend your first week on it.

## Step 4: Gather evidence that isn't opinions

Good evidence is about the past and present, not hypothetical futures.

**Ask about behavior, not opinions.** "Tell me about the last time a client sent revisions" beats "Would you use a revision tool?" The first gets you a real story with real details. The second gets you politeness.

**Mine public complaints.** Search the forums, subreddits and groups where your segment spends time. Look for people describing the problem in their own words. Save the exact phrasing — it becomes your marketing copy later.

**Look at what people already pay for.** Spending on workarounds (tools, freelancers, templates) is one of the strongest signals that a problem is real.

**Ask for a small commitment.** A paid pilot, a pre-order or even a scheduled follow-up call is worth more than a dozen compliments.

## Step 5: Score the evidence against your kill conditions

At the end of the test window, go back to what you wrote in Step 2 and score each clause honestly:

- **Passed:** the evidence clearly beat the kill condition.
- **Failed:** the kill condition was hit.
- **Unclear:** not enough signal.

Then decide:

- **Problem or Willingness to pay failed** → stop, or go back to Step 1 with a different segment.
- **Segment or Alternative failed** → the pain may be real for someone else. Narrow or shift the segment and re-test.
- **Everything passed** → build the smallest thing that solves the problem for the people you talked to, and sell it to them first.

An honest "no" after two weeks is a good outcome. It's months of building you didn't waste.

## A quick checklist

- [ ] Idea written as a five-clause hypothesis
- [ ] Segment specific enough that you know where to find them
- [ ] Kill conditions written down for every clause, before any research
- [ ] Riskiest clause identified and tested first
- [ ] Evidence is about real past behavior, not opinions
- [ ] Final verdict scored against the kill conditions you set
